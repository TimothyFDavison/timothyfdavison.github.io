## I Need To Grow Away From These Roots
This is an ambient music generation project inspired by the audio component of David Whiting's artistic piece,
[I Need To Grow Away From These Roots](https://www.vitling.xyz/i-need-to-grow-away-from-these-roots/). All creative credit should be directed to the original artist.

All code can be found on my Github [here](https://github.com/TimothyFDavison/I-Need-To-Grow-Away-From-These-Roots). 

Examples of the produced soundscapes - which are synthesized 
chords constructed mathematically rom waveforms and which progress on a random walk described later in this article - 
are provided below.

### Background
I'm an avid musician. Occasionally, I see an intersection of my tech and music hobbies which captures 
my interest. David Whiting's piece blends music theory with a calming synchronized light display built 
in the shape of a tree. I'm a huge fan.

His project page included a loose recipe for how he played with music theory - specifically, the chord progression and 
accent note selection and duration - to produce the soundscape. The recipe was specific enough to provide clear 
guidelines for reproduction, but general enough that no two implementations are likely to be identical.

I decided to implement the music generation component and explore the chord progression via some basic network
visualization. I may eventually build the tree component using LED strips and an Arduino or Raspberry Pi, if 
time and apartment space allows.

### Implementation
The original artist provided the following "score":

```
Enumerate every possible Major, Minor, Major 7th and Minor 7th chord and all possible inversions, 
beginning with A2 and ending with D5.
Choose a random chord from the set.
Play the chosen chord for 8 seconds
Find all the chords from the set that have all but one note in common with the playing chord.
Choose one of these at random.
Go back to 3 and repeat forever.

Play a note from the currently playing chord in (A) on a random waveform for a random duration - repeat a random
amount of times if the duration is short.
Wait a random amount of time.
Go back to 1 and repeat forever

Whenever a new chord is triggered by (A), illuminate each stem from root to tip with a colour corresponding to the 
each note of the chord. Notes adjacent in the cycle of 5ths have similar hues.

Whenever a note is played by (B), illuminate the tip of one of the stems with a light colour corresponding to the note.
```
I found that the chord progression setup lent itself very naturally to a random walk through a chord graph, in which 
chords represented nodes and shared notes represented edges. I used `networkx` to produce that graph
based on some parameters of interest, e.g. inclusion of major, minor, seventh, and diminished chords.

|       ![](/assets/graph-1.png)        | 
|:-------------------------------------:|

I then used `numpy` to synthesize sine waves of musical notes based on their numerical frequencies, including adding 
some fade and stylization to the sound envelope. I sprinkled in some randomness in the octaves and durations of the
accent notes, and then used `multiprocessing` and  `simpleaudio` to concurrently play the base chords and the accents.

### Network Visualization
I decided to visualize my chord network to see how the musical landscape evolved over time. This decision led me down 
several rabbit holes on topological distance metrics and underlying mathematical relationships in musical structure.

Here's the critical question: **_how should we define similarity between notes or chords?_**

Sound is already mathematical - a note represents a frequency, and an octave change doubles or halves that
frequency. We tend to find certain ratios of these frequencies pleasing or displeasing, 
giving rise to chords and chord progressions. 

The standardization of A = 440 Hz as a tuning reference began in the early 20th century and was internationally 
adopted in the mid-century, though slight variations persist. If we had collectively decided that A were to be 439 Hz
instead of 440, our preferred ratios would remain the same, but all of today’s music would be slightly differently
pitched.

Two minor chords share an isomorphic structure — the same interval pattern — even when rooted at different frequencies.
Are A minor and C minor more similar to one another than A minor and A major? Should 440 Hz be considered similar to
439 Hz and 441 Hz, seeing as their tones are near identical? How do we quantify how different F4 minor and F5 minor
(identical chords separated by one octave) are from one another, and is that difference less significant than the 
difference between F4 minor and G4 minor?

|       ![](/assets/graph-2.png)        | 
|:-------------------------------------:| 
| *Reducing octave separation, increasing separation between notes.* |

These questions arise naturally from attempting to visualize our chord network, as the design of our distance metric 
will induce radically different topologies in the output graph. I spent some time playing around with different ways 
to quanitfy similarity 
and distance within this context, exploring different possible weights in a distance function for things like octave 
separation, frequency clusters, number of overlapping root notes between chords, etc. Some of these knobs and dials 
can be found in `config` files on my Github page, with accompanying screenshots from network structures emanating from 
those variations on the Github README.
